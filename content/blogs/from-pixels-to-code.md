Back in 2021, I started out as a self-taught graphic designer, at a time when I didn't even know the fundamental difference between raster and vector graphics. Photoshop was the only tool I knew. I genuinely believed Illustrator was just another clever marketing gimmick by Adobe to sell more software.

That mindset changed when my company required me to use Illustrator for a print design project. Working hands-on with print made the difference between raster and vector crystal clear, and it was my first real eureka moment in this field.

Even then, I was still an absolute beginner. Around that time, I stumbled upon a YouTube video about UI/UX design. I'm someone driven by intense curiosity by nature, and I couldn't resist diving in. I decided to teach myself UI/UX design from scratch.

## From Design to the Web

But my curiosity didn't stop at static mockups. As I designed layouts, a question kept nagging at me: _how does this actually turn into a real, functional website?_ That question led me to HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, the building blocks of the web. Discovering that these technologies breathe life into static pixels was the real turning point.

I found a full tutorial covering HTML, CSS, and JavaScript on YouTube and started working through it. But when the tutor reached the JavaScript section, they paused for a disclaimer:

> JavaScript is a real programming language. Before this, you should watch our programming fundamentals tutorial.

I took that seriously and went looking for it.

That fundamentals series had ten videos: five on C, five on Java. I managed to grasp the basics, but it felt brutally difficult. Instead of scaring me off, the difficulty woke up my curiosity further. I started researching other languages and stumbled onto one that instantly clicked: Python. The funny part is that through all of this late-night exploration, I was still working my regular day job as a graphic designer. But Python's clean syntax and readability hooked me, and I dove headfirst into learning it properly.

## Falling for Python

I fell completely in love with it. Guided by YouTube tutorials, I built simple terminal games like Hangman and a handful of small CLI projects. Watching lines of text on a black terminal screen turn into playable logic felt like pure magic, and it cemented my passion for programming for good.

Having started with frontend fundamentals, arriving at Python opened my eyes to something bigger: you could build full websites with it too. Naturally, I turned to Django. But jumping straight in felt overwhelming. The folder structures, the boilerplate, the architectural concepts were simply over my head at that stage, and I couldn't see how it all fit together.

That wall put my programming journey on hold. For the next few years, I stepped back from code entirely and put my full focus back into design, switching across two companies and sharpening my creative skills along the way. Just when I thought code was behind me, life had another turn waiting.

## An Unexpected Return

At the creative agency I was working at, discussions began about building websites for clients, but the team had no real experience with web workflows, custom-coded or WordPress. My earlier explorations, even if theoretical, meant I understood how web development actually worked. When I stepped up to walk the leadership and team through modern development practices versus WordPress, they were genuinely impressed.

We had a WordPress developer on the team, but he lacked hands-on confidence. Even though I wasn't shipping production code daily, my conceptual clarity turned out to be exactly what the team needed. In my free time, I dusted off my old web development knowledge and dove back in. Until the agency could hire a dedicated development team, I stepped into the gap, supporting the WordPress developer, structuring technical roadmaps, and sitting in on client meetings to handle the technical side.

Eventually the agency hired a proper development team, and my role as the interim jack-of-all-trades came to a natural end. But by then, the coding bug had bitten for good. There was no turning back.

## No Turning Back

From that point on, it became an obsessive, solo self-learning journey. I circled back and finally conquered Django. I deepened my frontend skills across HTML, CSS, JavaScript, React, and Next.js. Python stayed at the core of everything I built (CLI tools, small utilities), and eventually I became an Arch User Repository (AUR) maintainer.

Fast forward to today: I built and launched [Cashevide](https://cashevide.com), a platform designed to help freelancers manage invoicing and client relationships. It's built from scratch with Django, Django REST Framework, and React, fully containerized with Docker, and self-hosted on a VPS. It's the largest thing I've shipped end to end, and the first time all these scattered years of self-teaching came together into something real people can use.

And the wildest part? By day, I'm still a 9-to-6 senior graphic designer.

Despite everything I've built, learned, and deployed, I know in my heart that I'm still a beginner in this vast universe of software engineering. There's so much logic left to master, so many systems left to architect, so many heights left to conquer. But if this journey has taught me one thing, it's that relentless curiosity will always turn pixels into code.
